## The creation cycle

Creating a good material with AI isn't "writing an instruction and copying the result". It's a six-step cycle:

1. **Plan.** Define the learning objective, describe your students (in general terms, with no personal data) and decide what you'll adapt and based on what.
2. **Ask.** Write the prompt with the six ingredients from Step 4.
3. **Review.** Read the result with a critical eye: is it correct, relevant and appropriate? (Step 6).
4. **Adjust.** Ask the AI for changes or edit it yourself.
5. **Apply.** Use the material in class and watch how your students respond.
6. **Reflect.** What worked? What would you change? Save the prompts that gave good results.

<Callout type="idea">
This cycle is a form of **reflective practice**: formulate, evaluate and reformulate, just as you do when you plan a lesson. It also follows the logic of action research: **plan, act, observe and reflect**, and then start again with a better material.
</Callout>

## A complete example: the water cycle

**Situation:** a grade 7 natural science teacher at a public school, with 32 students. After a diagnostic activity, she identified three groups:

- a group that still confuses evaporation with condensation;
- a group that has mastered the basics;
- a group that already connects the cycle with weather phenomena.

There is also **a student with low vision** in the class.

### 1. Plan

- **Objective:** explain the stages of the water cycle and relate them to everyday phenomena.
- **What she adapts:** the content and the process.
- **Based on what:** readiness (three levels) and learning profile (accessibility for low vision, following UDL).
- **Shared product:** at the end, everyone will explain the cycle with a diagram of their own.

### 2. Ask

<PromptExample label="First prompt">
Act as a secondary school natural science teacher with experience in Universal Design for Learning.

Context: grade 7, students aged 12 and 13 at a public school in Bucaramanga, Colombia. The group has three levels of readiness on the topic: some confuse evaporation and condensation, others have mastered the basics and others already connect the cycle with the weather. There is a student with low vision.

Learning objective: explain the stages of the water cycle and relate them to everyday phenomena.

Task: create a worksheet in three versions with the same objective:
- Version 1: with more support, short sentences, a 5-word glossary and examples from home (the boiling pot, the cold glass that "sweats").
- Version 2: expected level, with a short reading and 5 questions.
- Version 3: a challenge that connects the water cycle with the rainy seasons in Santander.

Also, create an accessible version of version 2 for the student with low vision: one that doesn't depend on images, with descriptions in words, and suitable for reading aloud.

Format: for each version, include a title, a reading, activities and a closing question. Use clear language and examples from the Colombian context.
</PromptExample>

### 3. Review

The teacher read the result and noted:

- The stages were well explained, but version 3 mentioned **sublimation** without explaining it. Is it necessary for this grade? She decided it was, but with a definition.
- Version 1 used the word **"precipitation"** without explaining it.
- A figure about "the percentage of fresh water on the planet" had no source. She **checked** it in a reliable source before keeping it.
- The examples from home worked very well for her context.

### 4. Adjust

<PromptExample label="Follow-up messages">
"In version 1, explain 'precipitation' in simple words and add an example from daily life."
"In version 3, add a short definition of 'sublimation'."
"In the accessible version, describe each stage of the cycle in words, as if you were explaining it to someone over the phone."
</PromptExample>

After these adjustments, the teacher edited a few details by hand: she replaced place names with others close to the school and increased the font size of the printed version.

### 5. Apply

She handed out the versions without labels like "low level" or "high level". Instead, she used neutral names: **cloud path, river path and ocean path**. During the class, she watched who needed more support and who finished quickly.

<Callout type="consejo">
Avoid labels that compare students. Use neutral names or colors for the versions. What matters is that each student gets the support they need, without feeling singled out.
</Callout>

### 6. Reflect

When she finished, the teacher asked herself:

- Did everyone reach the objective? How do I know?
- Did any version turn out too easy or too hard?
- What would I change in the prompt next time?

She saved the final prompt, with her notes, in her **prompt bank**.

<Callout type="consejo" title="Build your prompt bank">
Open a document and save the prompts that worked for you, organized by topic and grade, with a note on what you would adjust. Next time you'll only have to change the details in square brackets. In Step 8 you'll find templates to get started.
</Callout>

<Resumen>
- Creating with AI is a **cycle**: plan, ask, review, adjust, apply and reflect.
- AI takes part in two of the six steps (ask and adjust); **the rest depend on your judgment**.
- Save your best prompts in a **bank** so you can reuse and improve them.
</Resumen>
