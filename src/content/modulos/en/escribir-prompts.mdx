## What is a prompt?

A **prompt** is the instruction you give the AI. It isn't just a question: it's a **carefully thought-out instruction** that guides what the tool is going to produce.

**Prompt engineering** is the skill of designing, adjusting and improving those instructions. Small changes in how you ask for something produce big differences in the result. It isn't an innate skill: it's learned with practice and clear criteria.

<Callout type="idea">
AI doesn't know your class. **Anything you don't tell it, it will assume.**
</Callout>

## The 6 ingredients of a good educational prompt

| # | Ingredient | Question it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Role** | Who do I want the AI to be? | "Act as a primary school math teacher with experience in inclusive education." |
| 2 | **Your students' context** | Who is the material for? | "Grade 5, 28 students aged 10 and 11 at a public school in Bucaramanga, Colombia. Several still confuse the numerator and the denominator." |
| 3 | **Learning objective** | What should they achieve? | "For them to recognize equivalent fractions." |
| 4 | **Task** | What material do I need? | "Create an exercise sheet with three levels of difficulty." |
| 5 | **Format** | How do I want to receive it? | "A table with the columns level, exercise and hint. One page per level at most." |
| 6 | **Criteria and constraints** | What must it meet or avoid? | "Simple language, everyday examples from Colombia, no answers included." |

<Callout type="consejo">
You don't always need all six. For a quick question, the task is enough. But to create a class material, **the more ingredients you include, the less you'll have to correct**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="cuidado">
"Your students' context" is a **description of the group**, never personal data. Don't write names, grades or diagnoses (see Step 7).
</Callout>

## Before and after

### Example 1: math, primary school

<BeforeAfter>
<PromptExample variant="weak">
Make me a fractions worksheet.
</PromptExample>
<PromptExample variant="strong">
Act as a primary school math teacher with experience in inclusive education.
My students are in grade 5 (ages 10 and 11), at a public school in Bucaramanga, Colombia. Several still confuse the numerator and the denominator.
Objective: for them to recognize equivalent fractions.
Create an exercise sheet with three levels:
- Level 1: with drawings and a worked example.
- Level 2: exercises with the number line.
- Level 3: an open problem from everyday life.

Present it in a table with the columns level, exercise and hint. Use simple language and examples from Colombia (arepas, pesos, the neighborhood corner store). Don't include the answers.
</PromptExample>
</BeforeAfter>

**What changed?** The second prompt says *who* it's for, *what* it's for and *how* it should look. The result comes out much closer to what you need and only requires minor adjustments.

### Example 2: language arts, secondary school

<BeforeAfter>
<PromptExample variant="weak">
Write questions about a short story.
</PromptExample>
<PromptExample variant="strong">
You are a grade 8 language arts teacher.
My students read the short story I've pasted below. Some have difficulty making inferences.
Objective: develop inferential comprehension.
Create 9 questions about the story: 3 literal, 3 inferential and 3 critical. For each inferential question, add a hint that helps students find the answer in the text.
Format: a numbered list, grouped by question type.
Base your questions only on the text I give you.
[Paste the story here]
</PromptExample>
</BeforeAfter>

Notice the last instruction: **"base your questions only on the text I give you"**. When you give the AI the original material, you reduce the risk of it making up details.

### Example 3: natural sciences, secondary school

<BeforeAfter>
<PromptExample variant="weak">
Make me a quiz on photosynthesis.
</PromptExample>
<PromptExample variant="strong">
Act as a grade 7 natural sciences teacher.
My students are 12 and 13 years old. Many believe plants get their food from the soil.
Objective: check whether they understand that plants make their own food from light, water and carbon dioxide.
Create a 6-question multiple-choice quiz with 4 options each. The wrong options should reflect common student mistakes, like the one I mentioned.
Format: first the questions, ready to print. Then, in a separate section for me, the correct answer and what mistake each wrong option reveals.
Use simple language. At the end, list the scientific claims I should verify.
</PromptExample>
</BeforeAfter>

Here the prompt uses **what you already know about your class**: the mistake that keeps coming up. That way, every wrong answer tells you which idea needs more work, and the final list reminds you to check the facts before you use the quiz.

### Example 4: social studies, upper secondary

<BeforeAfter>
<PromptExample variant="weak">
Make me a rubric for a debate.
</PromptExample>
<PromptExample variant="strong">
You are a grade 10 social studies teacher.
My students will debate in teams about water use in Santander: what should come first, household use, farming or mining.
Objective: that they argue with evidence and respond respectfully to the opposing side.
Create a rubric with 4 criteria: use of evidence, clarity of the argument, response to the other side and respect in the dialogue.
Format: a table with 4 levels (superior, high, basic and low). In each cell, describe what you can observe, not just "good" or "fair".
Write it in language my students can understand, because I'll give it to them before the debate.
</PromptExample>
</BeforeAfter>

The key is asking for **each level to describe what you can observe**. A rubric that only says "excellent" or "fair" doesn't help anyone. And because you said you'll share it, the AI pitches the language to your students instead of to other teachers.

## The CLEAR framework: five qualities of a good prompt

Lo (2023) suggests checking your prompts against five qualities:

- **C**oncise: clear and direct, with no filler words.
- **L**ogical: well ordered. First the context, then the task and finally the format.
- **E**xplicit: it says exactly what you want (length, level, format).
- **A**daptive: you adjust it based on the answer you get.
- **R**eflective: you critically evaluate the result and learn for next time.

## Have a conversation: the first result is a draft

The first result is rarely the final one. The great advantage of these tools is that you can **keep the conversation going** to improve the material without starting from scratch. For example:

<PromptExample label="Follow-up messages">
"Level 1 is too hard. Add a worked example and a hint to each exercise."
"Change the examples so they're about soccer and the neighborhood corner store."
"Turn the explanation into a two-column table."
"Check your answer: is there any math mistake?"
"Make it shorter: 150 words at most."
</PromptExample>

## Strategies that work

Researchers have identified prompt "patterns" that solve common problems (White et al., 2023). These are the most useful for teachers:

| Strategy | What it's for | How to ask for it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role** | Getting answers from a specific expertise. | "Act as a school counselor who is an expert in inclusive education…" |
| **Audience** | Adjusting the language to the reader. | "Explain it for an 8-year-old." / "Write it to send to families." |
| **Ask me first** | Having it gather the information it needs before creating anything. | "Before you create the material, ask me any questions you need about my students, one at a time." |
| **Template** | Always getting the same structure. | "Use exactly this structure: title, objective, activity, wrap-up." |
| **Checklist** | Knowing which facts to check. | "At the end, list the facts and claims I should verify." |
| **Reflection** | Understanding its decisions. | "Explain why you organized the activity this way and what you assumed about my students." |

<Callout type="consejo" title="If you don't know where to start">
Use the **"ask me first"** strategy. Write: *"I want to create a material for my class. Before you do, ask me all the questions you need, one at a time"*. The AI will guide you, and you just answer.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- **Asking for everything in one huge, confusing message.** It's better to split the task into steps.
- **Not saying the grade or age.** The level of the language and the examples will be left to chance.
- **Not stating the objective.** You'll get a nice-looking material with no clear purpose.
- **Accepting the first answer without reviewing it.** It's always a draft.
- **Including students' personal data.** Describe needs, not people.
- **Asking for "something creative" without criteria.** Say what you mean by creative: a game, a story, a challenge.

<Resumen>
- A good educational prompt has **6 ingredients**: role, your students' context, objective, task, format and criteria.
- Check your prompts with **CLEAR**: concise, logical, explicit, adaptive and reflective.
- **Talk and adjust**: the first result is a draft.
- If you don't know where to start, ask the AI to **ask you first**.
</Resumen>
