## The core principle: people at the center

UNESCO proposes a **human-centered approach**: technology should serve educational goals, not the other way around. In practice, this means AI can help you produce, but **the teaching decisions are yours**: what to teach, how to assess, how to support each student.

## Six commitments for responsible use

### 1. Protect your students' data

**Never** enter into an AI tool:

- your students' names, ID numbers or photos;
- grades, marks or disciplinary notes;
- medical or psychological diagnoses;
- family or personal situations.

In Colombia, **Law 1581 of 2012** (Ley 1581 de 2012) protects personal data, and children's and adolescents' data has special protection. Also, many tools may use your conversations to train their models: check the settings and turn that option off if you can.

Describe **needs**, not people:

<PromptExample variant="weak" label="Avoid">

</PromptExample>
<PromptExample variant="strong" label="Better">
Create a math worksheet for a grade 6 student who needs short instructions, frequent breaks and visual support to stay focused.
</PromptExample>

### 2. Be transparent

Tell your students (and their families, when relevant) that you used AI to create a material. That way you model the honest behavior you expect from them. You can add a footnote like this one:

> This material was created with the help of generative artificial intelligence and reviewed by the teacher.

### 3. Verify before sharing

Every material must go through your review before it reaches the classroom. You're still responsible for what you teach (see the checklist in Step 6).

### 4. Watch for bias and look after diversity

AI can reproduce stereotypes: scientists who are always men, families of only one kind, regions or groups described with prejudice. **Ask for diversity explicitly** ("include characters who are diverse in gender, region and background") and review the result.

### 5. Respect authorship

If you use other people's texts, images or ideas, cite them. In academic settings, don't present AI-generated text as your own without saying so. Find out whether your institution has policies on the use of AI.

### 6. Think about equity

Not all your students have a computer or internet access at home. AI can widen the **digital divide** if assignments depend on it. Design materials that work **printed or offline**, and don't make an assignment depend on students having access to an AI tool.

<Callout type="idea">
AI is more useful as **a teacher's tool** for preparing better materials than as a requirement for students to be able to learn.
</Callout>

## What if my students use AI?

- **Respect the minimum age.** UNESCO recommends that generative AI not be used in the classroom before age 13, and each tool has its own minimum age (see Step 3).
- **Teach them to use it with judgment:** check facts, cite sources, don't copy and be wary of anything that sounds too sure of itself.
- **Design activities where the process matters:** explaining in their own words, connecting with their experience, presenting orally, showing drafts.
- **Agree on clear rules:** when AI can be used, what for, and how to say that it was used.

## The Colombian framework

- **Law 1581 of 2012:** personal data protection.
- **Law 1341 of 2009 (ICT Law):** promotes teacher training in information technologies.
- **CONPES Document 4144 of 2025, National Artificial Intelligence Policy:** guides the ethical, responsible and sustainable use of AI in the country through 2030, and includes training people, teachers among them.

## Beyond this guide: your AI competencies

In 2024, UNESCO published an **AI competency framework for teachers**. It organizes what a teacher needs to know into five dimensions:

1. A human-centered mindset.
2. The ethics of AI.
3. AI foundations and applications.
4. AI pedagogy.
5. AI for professional development.

Each dimension has three levels of progression: **acquire, deepen and create**. This guide helps you take the first steps, especially in ethics and in AI pedagogy.
