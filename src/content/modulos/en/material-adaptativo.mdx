## First: what is a teaching material?

A teaching material is any resource **ready to use in class** (a reading, a study guide, a workshop, a presentation, a video, a game or an assessment) that carries **your learning objectives** inside it.

When you design a material, you make many decisions: what to teach, in what order, with what examples and for which students. That's why **designing a material is planning your teaching**, not just "making a photocopy".

## And when is it adaptive?

A material is **adaptive** when it's designed, or deliberately adjusted, to respond to the characteristics of the learner: one student or a group.

<Callout type="cuidado">
Having "several versions" isn't enough. A version is adaptive only if it **changes something specific** (the content, the process or the product) **for a specific reason** (your students' readiness, interests or learning profile).
</Callout>

## The adaptation matrix

Differentiated instruction (Tomlinson, 2014) asks you to answer two questions: **what am I going to adapt?** and **based on which characteristic of my students?**

### What can I adapt?

| What I adapt | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Content** | What the student learns, or how they access the information. | A reading about the water cycle written at three levels of complexity. |
| **Process** | How they work to understand. | Some solve problems with hands-on materials, others with written problems and others with an open challenge. |
| **Product** | How they show what they learned. | Choosing between a poster, an explanatory audio recording or a written report. |

### Based on which characteristic?

| Characteristic | Guiding question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Readiness** | What do they already know, and what are they missing? | Exercises with more or fewer supports: hints, worked examples, guided steps. |
| **Interests** | What motivates them? | Fractions explained with soccer, cooking recipes or music. |
| **Learning profile** | How do they work best? | The same information as text, as a visual diagram or as audio. |

<Callout type="consejo">
When you ask AI for something, always name both things: **what** you want to adapt and **based on what**. For example: "Adapt the *content* to my students' *readiness level*…". You'll see this in Step 4.
</Callout>

## Designing for everyone from the start: UDL

**Universal Design for Learning** (UDL) proposes that flexibility shouldn't be a "patch" added at the end, but something planned from the beginning. Its most recent guidelines (CAST, 2024, version 3.0) are organized into three principles:

| Principle | In plain words | Ideas for your materials |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multiple means of **engagement** | The *why* of learning: sparking interest and sustaining effort. | Connect with your students' interests and identities, clear goals, options to choose from, achievable challenges. |
| Multiple means of **representation** | The *what*: presenting information in several ways. | Text with images and audio, a glossary of difficult words, concrete examples, highlighted key ideas. |
| Multiple means of **action and expression** | The *how*: letting students show what they learned in different ways. | Answering in writing, orally, with drawings or models; step lists to help them get organized. |

<Callout type="idea">
This is where AI helps enormously: producing the same idea in several formats (text, diagram, audio script, easy-to-read version) no longer takes hours, but minutes.
</Callout>

## Why does it work? Three key ideas

- **Just-right scaffolding (Vygotsky).** Between what a student can achieve alone and what they can achieve with help, there is a "zone of proximal development". A good material offers **just the right help** to move forward: not so easy that it bores, not so hard that it frustrates.
- **Connecting with what they already know (Ausubel).** Real learning happens when new ideas are anchored in prior knowledge and in the student's reality. That's why adapting isn't just changing the difficulty: it's also using **examples from their context**.
- **Learning in networks (Siemens).** Today we also learn by connecting sources, people and digital tools. Technology can help open **different paths** for different students.

## An example: one topic, three versions

**Topic:** equivalent fractions, grade 5.
**Objective (the same for everyone):** recognize that two different fractions can represent the same amount.

- **Version A, with more support.** Arepas (Colombian corn cakes) cut into parts, with drawings. One worked example, step by step, and four guided exercises, each with a hint.
- **Version B, expected level.** A short explanation using the number line, six exercises and a question asking students to explain in their own words.
- **Version C, challenge.** An open problem: "A recipe for 4 people uses 2/4 of a cup of sugar. How much do you need for 8 people? Is there another way to write it?". At the end, each student makes up their own problem.

All three versions pursue **the same objective**; what changes is the scaffolding. In terms of the matrix: the **content** and the **process** are adapted based on **readiness**.

<Callout type="idea">
Adapting isn't "lowering the bar". All students work toward the same objective, with different supports.
</Callout>

<Resumen>
- A teaching material **carries your learning objectives inside it**: designing it is planning.
- It's **adaptive** when it changes something specific (content, process or product) for a specific reason (readiness, interests or learning profile).
- **UDL** invites you to offer, from the start, several means of engagement, representation and expression.
- Adapting means giving **just-right scaffolding** and **connecting with what your students already know**, without lowering the objective.
</Resumen>
