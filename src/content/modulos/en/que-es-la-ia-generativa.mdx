## In a nutshell

**Generative artificial intelligence** (GenAI) is a type of artificial intelligence that **creates new content**: text, images, audio, video or code. It does this from an instruction that you write. That instruction is called a **prompt**.

Some well-known tools are ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot and Claude.

<Callout type="idea">
Generative AI doesn't "look up" a stored answer: it **builds** one every time you write to it. That's why it can be very useful… and also why it can get things wrong.
</Callout>

## How does it work? An analogy

Think of the autocomplete on your phone's keyboard: it suggests the next word based on what you've already typed. Generative AI does something similar, but on an enormous scale. It learned patterns from millions of texts and images and uses them to build the answer that seems to fit your instruction best.

Two important ideas follow from this:

- **It writes with great confidence, even when it isn't right.** Its goal is to produce an answer that *sounds* good, not to check that it's true.
- **What you get depends on what you ask for.** A vague instruction produces a generic result; a clear instruction produces a more useful material.

Some tools only work with text. Others, called **multimodal**, understand and produce several formats at once: for example, you can upload a photo of an exercise or a PDF document and ask the tool to adapt it.

## What can it do for you as a teacher?

- Create **versions of the same material** for students at different levels.
- Suggest **examples and analogies** connected to your students' interests.
- Write **drafts** of study guides, workshops, readings, quizzes and rubrics.
- Suggest **activity ideas** and lesson sequences.
- Adjust the **language** of a text: simpler, more technical or in easy-to-read format.
- **Transform content** into another format: an outline, a table, a concept map or a script for a video.

<Callout type="consejo">
Think of AI as an **assistant that writes drafts very quickly**. The teaching design (what to teach, to whom and why) is still yours. AI doesn't replace your judgment: it amplifies it.
</Callout>

## What generative AI doesn't do well

- **It can make up information.** This is called a *hallucination*: facts, dates, quotes or references that sound real but don't exist.
- **It can repeat biases and stereotypes** present in the data it learned from.
- **It doesn't know your students.** It doesn't know their grade, their context, their interests or their difficulties. If you don't tell it, it guesses.
- **Its information may be out of date**, because it learned from data up to a certain date (unless the tool searches the internet).
- **It personalizes superficially if no one guides it.** Without a teacher's mediation, "adaptation" usually stops at making the text easier or harder.

## Myths and realities

| Myth | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "AI always tells the truth." | It can be wrong with complete confidence. Everything it produces must be checked. |
| "AI is going to replace teachers." | AI produces drafts. The teacher designs, decides, adapts, supports and assesses. |
| "You need to know how to code to use it." | You use it by writing in everyday language, as in a chat. What does take practice is learning to ask well. |
| "If AI made it, it has no teaching value." | Its value depends on your teaching intent, on how you ask and on how you review it. |
| "The longer the answer, the better." | What matters is that it's correct, relevant and right for your students. |

## Why a guide?

Research shows that the main barrier to using AI in education isn't the technology, but the **lack of training** and of **clear pedagogical guidance**. Many teachers use it intuitively, "trying it and seeing what comes out". This guide offers you an orderly path for using it with pedagogical intent, critically and responsibly.

<Resumen>
- Generative AI **creates new content** from your instructions (prompts).
- It's fast and versatile, but **it can make things up, carry biases and ignore your context**.
- Its teaching value **depends on you**: on how you ask and how you review what it produces.
</Resumen>
